# Data-Science-Ops-and-Eng-Capstone

Metro Atlanta is surging as a premier demographic and economic engine in the United States. The city has reclaimed its spot as the 6th largest metropolitan area nationwide and has packed on nearly 400,000 new residents since 2020. New development often replaces trees and green space with pavement and buildings, raising urban temperatures. Tree canopies and vegetation cool neighborhoods, but planners have few tools to weigh these forces against one another. This project asks whether environmental characteristics can predict land-surface temperature at the neighborhood level. The resulting scenario testing tool would show planners how much added vegetation could offset the heat from new infrastructure, and what happens to predicted temperatures if development continues. This can guide where Atlanta prioritizes development and green infrastructure across neighborhoods. 
This project will use the Extract-Transform-Load (ETL) pipeline that integrates three spatial datasets into a unified tabular format. Within this format, each row represents a distinct Atlanta neighborhood or geospatial grid cell at a specific time. Our target variable, Land Surface Temperature (LST), is extracted from NASA satellite imagery. To engineer predictive features, we utilize the USGS National Land Cover Database to quantify tree canopy coverage alongside the percentage of artificial surfaces such as buildings, roads, and concrete, which the database classifies as developed land. NOAA meteorological data provides baseline climate control variables.
For the predictive analysis, the methodology progresses from foundational statistical modeling to machine learning techniques. We first train a linear regression model to establish a baseline relationship between the environmental inputs and surface temperatures. This baseline is vital for interpreting the direct significance of individual features in our data, such as the exact cooling coefficient of tree canopies versus the heating downside of concrete development. Building upon our first model, we will implement a Random Forest model for the purpose of capturing complex nonlinear interactions and spatial dependencies between the urban infrastructure and heat retention that a standard linear regression model misses. To validate our approach, we will compare the models using performance metrics of Mean Absolute Error (MAE), Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE), and R^2 to evaluate predictive accuracy. After the modeling phase, we will extract the most important features to identify which environmental factors most strongly influence temperature variability.
Our expected output will be an app or website displaying an interactive map of Atlanta divided by neighborhood. Users can click any area to see surface temperatures, heat risk levels, and key environmental drivers. Ideally, there will also be a built-in “what if” simulator showing how environmental changes, such as planting 50 new trees, removing a 1-acre parking lot, or adding a 4-acre park, affect temperature. This will be important for urban developers to evaluate the tradeoff between new construction and heat mitigation across different neighborhoods. For example, before adding new infrastructure, developers can use our tool to forecast predicted surface temperature after development or determine exactly how much additional vegetation would be needed to offset the added heat
